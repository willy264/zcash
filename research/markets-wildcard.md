# Zecathon research: Private Markets and Wildcard

Research date: **24 October 2026**. This is a product-research contribution, not an implementation audit. Technology statements below are tied to primary documentation; proposals and commercial hypotheses are labelled as such. Public project READMEs are evidence that an idea is already being attempted, not evidence that its privacy claims are correct.

## Conclusions for choosing a project

The strongest market opportunity is to make **private allocation produce an actually usable, unlinkable entitlement**, rather than stop at announcing an auction winner. **QuotaShade** does this for scarce API/compute capacity. **PatchPool** is the more unusual product: competing companies can collectively buy a software fix or license into the public domain without disclosing that they need it. **CohortBid** uses a procurement mechanism for sensitive research participants, with a visible compensation benchmark and hidden participants.

For Wildcard, **Fogbound** makes a hidden financial alliance graph part of a strategy game's rules; **Unowned Encore** turns private choices into live collective art without a patron leaderboard; **StreetProof** pays for useful local measurements while avoiding a permanent public graph of a contributor's earnings and movements. StreetProof has the strongest continuing utility; Unowned Encore has the clearest stage demo; Fogbound has the strongest explanation of why financial privacy changes the product.

All six can be prototyped without assuming native Zcash smart contracts, native NFTs, live Zcash Shielded Assets, or atomic delivery of off-chain goods. For the current hackathon build window, a small trusted coordinator with explicit boundaries is a feasible starting architecture. However, a coordinator that can read bids must be described as such: shielded settlement alone does not make the matching process private from the operator.

### A practical shared transaction path

1. The client locally generates an ephemeral signing key and random request identifier. A short memo binds the payment to that identifier; subsequent off-chain requests are signed by that key.
2. A current wallet pays a shielded address via a tested ZIP-321 URI. The application never asks the customer for a seed phrase.
3. A dedicated incoming-viewing wallet service scans payments using a current compatible wallet backend and light-client connection. The public UI does not receive viewing keys.
4. The app matches **a decrypted output plus amount plus expected memo plus confirmation policy**, not a public transaction ID alone. The same transaction/output cannot be credited twice. Block reorganizations and invoice expiry have explicit states.
5. Off-chain payloads are authenticated and encrypted where needed. A publicly posted hash of a low-entropy bid or answer is not private: commitments need a fresh, large random nonce and domain separation.
6. A separate wallet authorizes refunds or payouts to explicit fresh shielded addresses. Its policy can be simple/manual in a hackathon, but the demo must show a genuine transaction and identify any custody.
7. Every blind-token issuance is a durable, exactly-once authorization: atomically bind the credited invoice or accepted submission to the digest of the blinded request and store its exact signature response before returning it. A retry of the same request returns the stored response; a changed request cannot spend the same credit again. Use a documented confirmation policy. A later chain reorganization can invalidate the funding after a token has been issued or spent; the issuer must explicitly bear/reserve for this residual credit risk rather than claim finality or silently revoke a whole anonymity epoch. Redemption also needs an atomic spent-token record and idempotent payout state, so a timeout after broadcast does not send a second payment.

The UI should show **who can see what**, not a single green “private” badge. No public wallet login, invoice identifiers in analytics, global viewing-key publication, public per-user payout table, or fresh cross-chain transaction immediately followed by a uniquely sized activity payment. Removing application logs does not erase a cloud provider's or network observer's visibility.

## Private Markets idea 1: QuotaShade

**Pitch:** Buy scarce compute capacity at a public clearing price, then consume it without exposing which bid, payment, or company produced the workload.

**Real user/problem.** A small AI company wants a short GPU batch, inference quota, or premium data API without broadcasting what it is building or its maximum willingness to pay. A provider has a fixed quantity of standardized capacity that will expire unused. Ordinary public order books leak demand; ordinary account-based auctions tie bidding history to later workloads. This is a capacity-allocation product, not merely a private checkout.

**Novel combination.** A uniform-price batch auction discovers the rate. Shielded settlement hides the wallet graph. Blind issuance converts each paid allocation into fixed-denomination, single-use quota credentials. The bidder can later redeem a credential against the provider without revealing the auction identity that won it. The product protects two different edges: the financial edge and the allocation-to-usage edge.

**Concrete first market.** Auction 20 identical 100-request API bundles for a single local image-resize or model-inference endpoint. A real endpoint is much more convincing than fake “GPU tokens”; no cloud-provider partnership is needed. Sellers do not need to be anonymous in this first version. The public promise is anonymous demand-side participants, and the UI must say so.

**Mechanism and architecture.**

1. Provider publishes the service definition, quantity, minimum rate, bid deadline, allocation/tie rules, and a pinned common token-issuer key for this service/epoch.
2. Each client submits a salted commitment and an encrypted bid `{quantity, max_price, ephemeral_claim_key}`. The MVP coordinator may decrypt bids; a full-privacy matching extension uses independently operated MPC and reveals only allocation and clearing price.
3. At close, the engine allocates the homogeneous bundles with a published uniform-price rule. Public output is the clearing price and sufficiently aggregated quantity. Each client privately receives its allocation/invoice through its claim key.
4. Winner pays provider directly in shielded ZEC. The scanner verifies the invoice. In exchange the provider signs exactly the number of blinded unit credentials that were purchased. No amount is trusted from inside the blind token; the signing key identifies a fixed quota unit.
5. Client unblinds, stores credentials locally, and redeems one later. The provider verifies its signature and atomically marks the random serial spent before serving the bounded quota. The issuer never saw that unblinded serial at checkout.

**Privacy boundary.** Public observers see a posted rate, aggregate market activity, and ordinary shielded transaction metadata, not bidder wallets or bids. The MVP auctioneer sees bids and allocations, but should only see ephemeral application keys. The provider sees a redeemed token and the actual API request; this is **not confidential computing**, so workload contents remain visible to the service. The issuer/provider can still correlate timing, unique batch sizes, browser metadata, or IPs. Uniform bundle sizes, shared epoch keys, delayed redemption, separate sessions/relays, and no account login reduce those links; they do not justify claiming network anonymity.

**Settlement and custody truth.** Direct winner-to-provider settlement avoids holding the purchase principal. A small fixed shielded bid bond, if included, is custodial until returned. Paying after the auction is not binding escrow: a winner may default, so implement a short expiry and a clearly defined next-bidder or reauction rule. Token delivery follows payment and is not atomically enforced on Zcash. The provider can deny service or overissue credentials; publish supply counters and make the demo prove the actual endpoint works.

**MVP and demo.** Five locally independent clients bid for three bundles; the public board reveals only one rate. Two winners pay actual testnet invoices, receive tokens, and redeem from fresh sessions. Show the payment database and redemption database side by side: no common token serial or wallet address. Replay one token and reject it. Demonstrate an unpaid winner expiring. A competitor watching the public board cannot learn which bidder performed the later inference request from the application records alone.

**Integrations.** Current Zcash wallet + ZIP 321; `librustzcash` scanner; Zebra/lightwalletd data source; RFC-9474 blind tokens. Optional NEAR Intents funding for users with other assets must happen as a separately disclosed funding step, not inside the anonymity claim. A FROST vault is unnecessary for this MVP.

**Risk/kill criterion.** If the team cannot get a real paid token through the endpoint on day one, reduce the auction to one quantity per bidder. Do not spend the remaining window writing an entire browser wallet or claiming MPC fairness from plaintext matching code. This idea overlaps accountless prepaid credits in the Payments track, but its distinctive value is scarce-resource price discovery and the separation of bidding history from usage.

## Private Markets idea 2: PatchPool

**Pitch:** Let competitors quietly pool demand to buy a fix, dataset, or software license into public availability.

**Real user/problem.** Several companies need the same obscure dependency fixed. Revealing that need discloses their stack, a strategic project, or security exposure. Individually they cannot justify the maintainer's full fee; collectively they could. Ordinary bounty pages and crowdfunding expose sponsors, donation sizes, and dependency graphs. The product is a market for **collective release of a digital deliverable**, with a public purchase price and private buyers.

**Distinctive mechanism.** A maintainer publishes a fixed buyout price and an encrypted deliverable committed before funding. Organizations make private, funded conditional pledges. Once the agreed threshold is met, the deliverable and its agreed license are released to everyone. Failed pools refund participants privately. This turns hidden demand into a nonexclusive product and deliberately avoids a public purchaser list or transferable membership token.

**Concrete first market.** A reproducible patch to a small open-source package or an openly releasable research dataset, with a test suite. Avoid live zero-day disclosures. Use a real maintainer-authored fixture whose utility the demo can verify.

**Mechanism and architecture.**

1. Seller posts a content hash, release terms, acceptance tests, buyout price, deadline, and encrypted archive. A named escrow/reviewer has the key and checks that the archive matches the promise before the pool opens.
2. Participants privately choose pledge sizes and pay a shielded escrow address with a short memo binding the pool and a random claim public key. No sender address is required or inferred.
3. The coordinator scans confirmed payments, sums qualifying commitments, and shows only coarsened progress or a threshold-crossed flag. Exact continuously updated totals can expose an individual pledge through differencing, even though no chain amount is public.
4. At the predeclared closing height, after the required confirmation depth, a funded pool releases payment to the seller and publishes the artifact key plus release record. If the threshold fails, the claimant signs a refund request with the bound ephemeral key and supplies a fresh shielded destination.
5. Public users download and run the released artifact. A participant receives a signed acknowledgment for its pledge without publishing its amount or deposit proof. This proves only the coordinator's acknowledgment; it does not prove that the private total, threshold decision, or eventual payout is correct.

**Privacy boundary.** The public sees the product, posted price, coarse threshold state, and eventual release. The coordinator sees pledge amounts and claim pseudonyms. The escrow may see those same records and the seller. A seller can remain a pseudonymous public maintainer, but the proposal does not promise that known companies can conceal their identity from an intermediary to whom they voluntarily reveal it. Return addresses are never placed in public logs. A dedicated pool viewing key is not a public audit mechanism: publishing it reveals sensitive pledges and memos.

**Settlement and custody truth.** The escrow holds participant funds and controls the release decision. A 2-of-3 threshold signer can reduce dependence on one key **only if operators are independent and current transaction signing works**; it does not make the off-chain release an atomic exchange. A public artifact hash proves byte identity, not usefulness or legal rights. Acceptance tests and pre-delivery escrow reduce seller failure; a buggy or dishonest escrow remains a risk. The release license must be explicit—publishing bytes does not buy copyright. Publish the funded threshold as seller price plus any disclosed platform fee; the operator separately prefunds a bounded network-fee reserve. Require the configured confirmation depth before a pledge counts. Define an exact closing height and a deterministic order for final pledges (inclusion height, transaction ID, output index): refund the portion above the target and refund valid-invoice payments first included after closure. Failed-pool refunds return the recognized principal, including any contingent platform fee; the operator covers transaction fees from its reserve. Define invoice minimums and limits so fee exposure is bounded, and clearly specify handling of unsolicited or below-minimum transfers. Lock a single durable settlement/refund decision to avoid racing both paths. Artifact publication is irreversible: if a later reorganization invalidates a counted contribution after release, the operator bears the shortfall rather than claiming that the artifact can be taken back.

**MVP and demo.** Three simulated organizations each privately pledge to fix a package. The public view never lists participants. One successful pool unlocks a patch and turns a failing reproduction green; a second unsuccessful pool returns one real shielded payment to a fresh address. Show a tampered artifact rejected before release. Display “coordinator can see pledges” in the operator view.

**Integrations.** ZIP-321 wallet invoices, memo-based claim binding, current wallet scanning, encrypted object storage, artifact hashes, and an ordinary signed release manifest. FROST is a stretch for threshold custody. NEAR Intents could fund the pool from different assets, but the pooled asset stays ZEC and the coordinator must not claim cross-chain payment routes are private from their providers. There is no dependency on ZSA or native escrow scripts.

**Why it is not generic crowdfunding.** The buyer is a set of mutually competing organizations; privacy allows them to reveal demand economically without revealing who needs the product. A committed, testable digital deliverable is bought out, so delivery is central. The maintainers receive a price for a defined asset, not an open-ended donation appeal.

**Risk/kill criterion.** High originality, moderate implementation complexity, material custody. Free-riding is a commercial risk because noncontributors receive the released public good too; the mechanism needs a small set of buyers with sufficient urgency rather than assuming universal voluntary contribution. If a real seller or releasable artifact cannot be found quickly, the demo becomes artificial. Do not call automatically scheduled refunds “trustless”; the wallet operator can still refuse to sign.

## Private Markets idea 3: CohortBid

**Pitch:** Recruit a sensitive research cohort at one publicly posted compensation rate without creating a public list of who qualified, answered, or got paid.

**Real user/problem.** A university, nonprofit, or product researcher wants 20 eligible respondents for research on employment, health access, or personal finances. Participants need compensation but may reasonably refuse a public crypto payment trail. Today a study often binds eligibility evidence, survey content, contact details, and payment records in one database. The market product is private **reverse procurement of research participation**, not a generic survey form.

**Novel combination.** Eligibility is checked once by an issuer, which blind-signs a one-use credential for a study cohort. Participants privately state minimum acceptable compensation. A reverse batch auction fills a fixed cohort with a single posted compensation rate. Qualified completed responses produce blind reward credentials that are later exchanged for shielded payouts. The study's allocation, response content, and payment destination therefore do not need to share a permanent identity.

**Concrete first market.** An anonymous study of software developers' experiences maintaining open-source dependencies. Eligibility is demonstrated to a study issuer through an existing community membership, not a new identity scheme. Health claims can be a future use case; the first demo should not collect real medical records.

**Mechanism and architecture.**

1. Researcher funds a capped study budget in a shielded payout wallet. Study policy defines a required cohort size, minimal eligible attributes, maximum compensation, quality rubric, release threshold, and public auction rule.
2. An eligibility issuer verifies membership and issues one blind credential under a common study/epoch key. The issuer knows who qualified but not the random credential serial later presented to the recruiter. It maintains its own “issued” record to limit duplicate credentials.
3. A respondent redeems eligibility once and submits a sealed minimum compensation bid bound to a fresh claim key. The recruiter selects the lowest acceptable bids and pays every accepted respondent the same published marginal accepted rate. The exact rule, ties, and consent are specified in advance; this is a market experiment, not a proven ethical or optimal compensation mechanism.
4. Selected participants submit responses through their one-use study session. After deterministic completion checks or review, they request a blinded fixed-value payout token. The quality reviewer sees the response, but not the future token serial.
5. After a delay/batch window, the participant unblinds and redeems the token at a payout service, providing a fresh shielded address. The payout service sees a token and destination but not the associated answer. A spent-token registry prevents replay.

**Privacy boundary.** Public users see study terms, the final compensation rate, and aggregated results only after a stated minimum cohort size. The eligibility issuer sees identities/eligibility evidence. The recruiter sees bid values and response content under temporary pseudonyms. The payout service sees payout addresses. Blind signatures remove a deterministic cryptographic mapping but do not stop timing/IP collusion, unique answer content, or a group of one. Common keys, standard payouts, batch issuance/redemption, minimal questions, and separate sessions are part of the design. A malicious issuer can create false eligible respondents; it is not proof of unique humanity. A minimum cohort size does not prove anonymity, prevent collusion, or provide differential privacy. The MVP should publish one predefined coarse statistical release per study, with no overlapping cohort queries, repeated filter refinements, or continuously changing exact counts; those interfaces enable differencing even when each output independently meets the size threshold.

**Settlement and custody truth.** The study operator holds its own reward budget; participants are not asked to deposit funds to qualify. Funding visibility can be attested selectively, but a shielded balance is not automatically a public solvency proof. Payment follows accepted participation and is not enforced by a native smart contract. Clear rejection rules and a private dispute receipt are necessary. Reward claims can be transferable bearer instruments unless separately bound to a locally generated key; no stronger nontransferability should be claimed.

**MVP and demo.** Eight synthetic qualified applicants, four study places, two bid values, one public clearing rate. Four responses arrive and only cohort statistics appear. Three users receive actual testnet payouts from fresh sessions. Try to join the eligibility table to payout records and show that the ordinary keys required for that join do not exist. Reject a reused eligibility token and a reused payout claim. Demonstrate a small cohort remaining suppressed rather than outputting identifying statistics.

**Integrations.** Zcash for rewards and budget, current wallet stack, RFC-9474 blind signatures for the two handoffs, encrypted off-chain response storage. Use separate key domains for eligibility and reward credentials. NEAR Intents is optional for researcher budget funding only. No FROST, ZSA, or novel zero-knowledge circuit is required for the core prototype.

**Risk/kill criterion.** This has a clear Monday buyer but requires consent and compensation design. Do not claim that bidding for lower compensation is automatically fairer to vulnerable people. If two blind-signing flows are too much, keep one unlinkable answer-to-payout flow and state that the recruiter then knows eligibility pseudonyms. Product differentiation comes from cohort procurement and private market pricing; a plain “anonymous survey that pays ZEC” is less distinctive.

## Wildcard idea 1: Fogbound

**Pitch:** A strategy game in which players can secretly bankroll alliances, and the public payment graph cannot give away the winning coalition.

**Real user/problem.** In a public-chain game, transferring resources to another faction can reveal alliances before the game does. Spectators can reconstruct patronage, bribery, and prize destinations from wallets. A social-deduction or diplomacy game has genuinely different mechanics when financial support stays hidden. The audience for the MVP is a six-person remote friend group or event, not a giant persistent metaverse.

**Novel mechanic.** A common board has three factions racing to repair a stranded city's infrastructure. Players submit simultaneous hidden plans and can anonymously buy a bounded support action for any faction. The board reveals only aggregate consequences at the end of each turn. Players can switch alliances privately. A small fixed sponsor-funded skill prize is paid shieldedly; no random lottery or token economy is needed.

**Architecture.** A browser generates an ephemeral game key. The authoritative game host publishes deterministic rules, a turn deadline, and a signed state hash. Each faction has a distinct host-controlled shielded receiving account and published payment request, so support is actually directed to that faction. Shielded payments contain short authenticated round/action commitments; complete signed plans are encrypted off-chain to the host. A host-scoped incoming watcher for each faction confirms qualifying payments. With transparent receiving accounts, the analogous public chain would directly identify the recipient faction and its funder; shielded outputs hide those edges. Merely paying a common host with encrypted game actions would not establish this difference. The host resolves a turn using the deterministic engine and publishes the new board and a receipt root. Each player can check that its committed action appears in the host's receipt tree with a private inclusion proof. That proof does not establish that all paid actions were included or that the host honestly computed the hidden-action aggregate. Public receipts must not contain private actions, addresses, or low-entropy unsalted hashes. At game end, players may opt to reveal signed action histories for a postmortem; they are not forced to reveal wallet history.

**Privacy boundary.** The public sees the board, turn totals, and final outcome. The chain does not directly disclose who funded a faction, but a small group can infer hidden actions from outcomes, known actions, or collusion. The host knows actions and temporary player keys and can collude or cheat; this is server-authoritative private gameplay, not a trustless hidden-state machine. A global network observer may correlate transaction timing to a player. Turn-wide batching, standard support amounts, separate faction receiver accounts, and no public transaction-to-action IDs help. If the host receives identities through a video call or account login, it knows those identities; make the claim narrower.

**Custody/settlement.** Support fees go to distinct faction-specific accounts controlled by the host; these are not player deposits under native escrow. Prizes come from a predeclared sponsor/host budget and are paid by its wallet. A refund policy applies to cancelled rounds. The host can fail to award a promised prize; FROST would change custody control, not validate game truth. A no-prize mode removes that liability and still demonstrates hidden patronage.

**MVP and demo.** Six browser windows, one three-turn map, three infrastructure choices, and separate receiving accounts for the factions. Two players secretly support rival factions with actual small testnet payments. Project the public board alongside an explicitly equivalent transparent-ledger comparison using separate publicly identified faction receivers: the transparent version exposes the payer-to-faction edges, while the shielded view does not. Use preconfirmed contributions for a short live presentation and visibly queue a fresh payment for the next turn; confirmation times make guaranteed instant on-chain turns unrealistic. Run the same deterministic disclosed-aggregate-to-board transition in a verifier and reject a board inconsistent with that disclosed aggregate. This checks the replayed state transition, not completeness of hidden inputs, honesty of the aggregate, or global game fairness. A player checks its inclusion receipt privately. Pay a single skill prize or explicitly use no-prize mode.

**Why this is distinct from Zeek.** Zeek's README describes paid treasure tiles and a random draw. Fogbound is about hidden coalitions, simultaneous strategy, and strategic financial information, with no randomness-driven payout mechanism. [S16] It should not copy Zeek's code while claiming newly authored originality.

**Integration fit.** ZIP-321 payment flow, current shielded memo scanner, deterministic Rust/TypeScript game engine, and optional wallet QR integration. The core should not be slowed down by cross-chain swaps or a new wallet. Sponsor-funded prize payment would be an organizational arrangement, not a currently verified official sponsor challenge.

**Risk.** Great pitch and demo, weaker business validation. Network confirmation time makes turn-based play appropriate; per-second on-chain actions are a bad fit. Do not disguise a centralized game host as decentralized consensus.

## Wildcard idea 2: Unowned Encore

**Pitch:** A live performance that the audience can privately commission together, with no rich-patron leaderboard and no record connecting a person's identity to their artistic choices.

**Real user/problem.** Independent artists need revenue and interaction, but public paid-vote systems often become a competition for visible status or expose sensitive preferences. A museum installation, live stream, or event can let people buy creative constraints without publishing which individual wanted which theme. Financial privacy becomes a creative rule: the work has patrons, but no publicly owned narrative.

**Novel mechanic.** The artist publishes three constrained branches for the next scene—sound, palette, and story direction—with a fixed price for one choice. Participants privately choose a branch via a shielded payment. The next scene derives from the **batch aggregate**, not a named donor or individual payment size. A person can commission a private prompt that contributes to a collectively generated texture, but raw prompt text is never automatically published. This is paid audience authorship rather than tipping.

**Architecture.** A local-first show controller exposes signed scene manifests and a ZIP-321 QR for each branch. The memo contains only a session/round ID, branch identifier, random nonce, and optional commitment to an encrypted off-chain prompt. A dedicated view-only scanner feeds a deterministic aggregation engine. The controller updates a browser canvas or projector, MIDI synth, or inexpensive LED display. It releases results only when a minimum payment count and round deadline are satisfied. The next scene and its aggregate input commitments are signed for replay. Small rounds are merged or refunded. A count of payments is not a count of distinct people; this is protection against trivial singleton disclosure, not Sybil-resistant k-anonymity.

**Privacy boundary.** The artist/controller sees branch choices and payments to its wallet, but ordinarily not sender addresses. The public sees only the resulting scene and sufficiently aggregated counts. A camera at the venue, a livestream username, a person announcing “I picked red,” or a uniquely timed payment can reveal choices. “No public payer-to-preference graph” is a defensible claim; “the artist cannot know your choice” is not. Exact per-transaction timestamps or contribution sizes should never be projected. An adversary who buys all but one choice can infer the remaining choice from a complete aggregate; limiting public output to the rendered scene and avoiding exact small counts reduces, but does not eliminate, this attack. An IPFS upload is not automatically private; encrypted prompt blobs need real encryption and reasonable deletion policy.

**Settlement/custody.** Payment is a fee for participation. If the minimum batch is not met, a private receipt capability requests a refund to a fresh shielded address. The show operator controls refunds and creative execution. A signed deterministic scene renderer can prove that a specified aggregate generated a scene, but does not prove that all private payments were honestly counted unless a further proof/independent check is implemented. Distinguish inclusion receipts from a complete tally proof.

**MVP and demo.** A 90-second staged performance driven by real shielded payments collected and confirmed before the presentation, with three audience clients, one soundscape, and one projected visual. A fresh payment submitted live is explicitly shown as pending and queued for a subsequent round after confirmation; do not claim it will confirm within the performance. For a genuine cold end-to-end session, allow a variable collection window of several minutes or longer. Nothing reveals individual choices until the configured threshold is reached; then the installation transforms. One private receipt proves inclusion. Replay the public scene from the recorded aggregate. Contrast this with a public tip leaderboard and show that the latter's donor-preference table simply does not exist in the product.

**Integration fit.** ZODL/other tested wallet URI, current viewing-wallet scanner, browser canvas/WebAudio, optional Raspberry Pi projector/LED controller. NEAR funding could broaden audience access but adds little to the core demo. Hardware is an output device, not a wallet that needs to hold a spending key.

**Risk.** This is the most creative stage demo, but commercial demand is a hypothesis. The differentiator must be commissioned changes to the performance and privacy of choices; a generic donation page plus generative art is too obvious. Give an actual artist a usable performance console rather than producing a one-off animation.

## Wildcard idea 3: StreetProof

**Pitch:** Pay people for useful city measurements without publishing a map of their wallet, daily route, and earnings.

**Real user/problem.** A local newsroom, university, or civic group needs measurements of heat, noise, power availability, or accessibility. Many tokenized sensor networks turn every contributor's actions and rewards into a permanent public economic graph. StreetProof separates **data people choose to publish** from the person's ongoing earning history.

**Novel mechanism.** A buyer posts a short-lived measurement bounty for a coarse area and time window. The participant collects a reading locally; the app strips persistent identifiers and unnecessary metadata, tests plausibility/calibration, and submits an encrypted payload. The validator approves a fixed-value blind reward claim. After a delay, that claim is redeemed for a shielded ZEC payout. The data map can remain public while the contribution-to-payout-wallet link is absent from ordinary application records.

**Concrete first instrument.** A calibrated thermometer or inexpensive sensor measuring an indoor heat/cooling demonstration; outside deployment is a future pilot. A phone microphone can provide illustrative noise readings but is not a certified sound-level meter. The demo should show actual sensor data rather than random values.

**Architecture.** A phone or ESP32 sends a measurement through a local browser gateway. The gateway attaches the requested coarse cell, bounded time bucket, calibration proof/record, payload commitment, and ephemeral request key. The validator checks the range, requested cell/time, duplicate submission identifiers, and optional agreement with a reference sensor. Accepted submissions receive one blind token under the shared fixed-reward/epoch key. The payout endpoint atomically spends the token and pays the supplied fresh shielded address. A separate publisher releases only aggregated cells with adequate contributions; raw traces and precise coordinates never go on-chain. The shielded memo can carry a bounty/receipt commitment for the buyer or recipient, but large datasets remain encrypted off-chain.

**Privacy boundary.** Public users see coarse area statistics, bounty policy, and possibly total payouts. The validator necessarily sees the submitted location/time and raw measurement, and may see the connection IP. The payout operator sees fresh addresses but not the originating unblinded submission serial. Neither blind signatures nor Zcash prevent location-based re-identification if one person repeatedly reports from a distinctive home/work route. Delay, coarsening, minimum cohorts, and no permanent device key are core requirements. Hardware attestation may improve provenance while making a sensor linkable: it must be disclosed as a tradeoff, not called anonymous by default.

**Truth and Sybil limitations.** A shielded payment proves payment, not that a measurement is real. A signature proves control of a device key, not that the sensor was at the claimed place. A blind token stops payout replay, not fake people, GPS spoofing, cloned sensors, or a malicious validator. The MVP can use an enrolled reference sensor and bounded bounty budget, with the resulting known-device privacy limit. A production version needs a separately validated measurement and abuse model; it should not pretend to solve this in a weekend.

**Settlement/custody.** The buyer funds the operator's reward budget. The operator verifies data and executes payouts. An on-chain hash does not enforce quality or force payment. The participant can show a signed acceptance receipt in a private dispute. Fixed rewards and batching prevent a unique payout amount from becoming a trivial identifying fingerprint, but timing leakage remains.

**MVP and demo.** Two real sensor readings plus synthetic clients clearly labelled for aggregation. Heat one sensor and watch a coarse map cell change only after the threshold. Approve the reading, issue a blind claim, redeem it later for an actual shielded payout, and reject the same claim a second time. Inspect the exported public dataset: no wallet, device serial, exact coordinate, IP, photo EXIF, or private request ID. Simulate a calibration failure and refuse the payout with a clear reason.

**Integration fit.** Sensor gateway + current Zcash wallet/backend + RFC-9474 claim issuance; Zebra as verification infrastructure. Optional NEAR Intents funding for a non-ZEC buyer has a clear role, but conversion metadata must be excluded from the contributor-privacy promise. FROST is unnecessary for a capped pilot budget. It is a real hardware/social application of Zcash rather than a new chain token.

**Risk.** Strong continuing usefulness and visual demo, but high truthfulness risk if the team claims sensor authenticity or Sybil resistance. The credible promise is “useful measurements paid without a public contributor earnings graph,” not a fully anonymous, fraud-proof decentralized sensor network.

## Comparative recommendation

These scores are editorial judgments, not competition predictions. 5 is strongest/easiest.

| Idea | Original combination | Monday usefulness | Four-day MVP feasibility | Demonstration clarity | Central risk |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| QuotaShade | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | Operator sees bids; service fulfillment remains trusted |
| PatchPool | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 | Custody plus deliverable/license enforcement |
| CohortBid | 4 | 5 | 3 | 4 | Eligibility trust, compensation design, metadata collusion |
| Fogbound | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Host sees hidden strategy; chain latency |
| Unowned Encore | 5 | 3 | 5 | 5 | Must be a useful artist tool, not a donation visualization |
| StreetProof | 4 | 5 | 3 | 5 | Measurement truth, location re-identification, Sybils |

**Recommended markets choice:** QuotaShade for a product-oriented engineering team; PatchPool for the more surprising pitch and a team with access to a maintainer or releasable artifact. **Recommended wildcard choice:** Unowned Encore if the team can produce a convincing artist experience; StreetProof if a real sensor and civic/data partner are available; Fogbound if the team has strong game-design execution.

For any choice: first complete a single genuine shielded payment → verified receipt → concrete product effect. Add richer cryptography after that path works. A dry run/simulation is useful but must be visibly labelled; no dummy chain events in the final working-path claim.
